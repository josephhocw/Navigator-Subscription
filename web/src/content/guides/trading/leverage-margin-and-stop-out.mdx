This is the page most people are nervous about, because leverage has a scary reputation. By the end of it you'll see it's just a way to use your money efficiently, and that the part with the frightening name, the stop-out, is there to protect you.

<GuideLevChain />

## Leverage: doing more with less of your cash

- **Leverage lets a small amount of your money control a larger trade.** With 1:200 leverage, every $1 of yours controls $200 of trade.
- You don't have to tie up all your cash in one position — put up a little and open a trade worth far more.
- Think of it like a deposit on a house. You don't pay the full price up front to control the whole property; you put down a fraction and control the rest.

<GuideCallout title="Does leverage mean more risk? No.">
This is the big myth, so let's clear it now. Leverage on its own doesn't make a trade risky. What sets your risk is how big your position is compared to your account, how far away you place your stop-loss, and how much the market itself tends to move.

A small, sensible trade at 1:200 leverage can be far safer than an oversized one at 1:20. The leverage number isn't the danger. The size you choose is.
</GuideCallout>

## Initial margin: the part held to open

- **Initial margin is the part of your money set aside to open the trade.** It isn't a fee and it isn't gone — it's held as a deposit, then released back to you when you close.
- **Initial margin = position size ÷ leverage.**
- Example: one lot of the Nasdaq 100 at 28,000, on 1:200 leverage → US$28,000 ÷ 200 = **US$140** held.

## Maintenance margin and your equity

- Your **equity** is your account's live value right now — your balance, plus or minus the profit or loss on any open trade.
- **Maintenance margin is the least equity you need to keep a trade open.** It's **half of the initial margin.**
- As a trade moves against you and your equity falls, the maintenance margin is the line you don't want to cross.

## Stop-out: the safety brake

- **If your equity falls below the maintenance margin, the platform closes the trade for you.** That automatic close is the stop-out.
- It isn't a punishment. It's a brake that stops a losing trade before it can run away from you.

## Full Example

Example: one lot of the Nasdaq 100 at 28,000, on 1:200 leverage.

| | Figure |
| --- | --- |
| Position size | US$28,000 |
| Leverage | 1:200 |
| **Initial margin** (28,000 ÷ 200) | **US$140** |
| **Maintenance margin** (half of that) | **US$70** |
| Stop-out | when your equity drops below US$70 |

Three steps to run before any trade:

1. **Work out the initial margin** — position size ÷ leverage. Here, US$28,000 ÷ 200 = US$140.
2. **Halve it for the maintenance margin** — the least equity that keeps the trade open. Here, US$70.
3. **Note the stop-out level, and keep enough funds to sustain the trade.** If your equity drops below US$70, the platform closes it for you.

<GuideCallout title="Can I lose more than I put in? No.">
With negative-balance protection, the worst case is the money in your account. You can't owe the broker more than you deposited. The stop-out makes that hold: it closes a losing trade before your balance can fall below zero.
</GuideCallout>

## Leverage isn't the same on every market

We recommend setting up your account at 1:200, but that's a ceiling — each instrument has its own leverage, and many are lower. As a rough guide:

| Market | Typical leverage |
| --- | --- |
| Major forex pairs | 1:200 |
| Gold and metals | 1:200 |
| Most indices | 1:200 |
| Hong Kong 50 | 1:100 |
| Popular stocks | up to 1:20 |
| Most other stocks | 1:10 |
| BTC/USD | 1:20 |

- A lower leverage just means more of your own money is held as initial margin to open the same-sized trade.
